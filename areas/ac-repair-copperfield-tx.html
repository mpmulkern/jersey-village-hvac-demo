What We See on Copperfield AC Systems

The Copperfield villages went up largely through the 1980s, which puts most homes on their second or third air conditioning system. That matters because the equipment tends to be reasonably modern while the infrastructure around it is not. We are regularly called to a ten-year-old system that has never cooled the back bedrooms properly, and the cause turns out to be forty-year-old flex duct in the attic that has crushed, separated at a boot, or lost its insulation value entirely.

Attic air handlers are near-universal here, so condensate problems are a standing feature of the area. A blocked drain in a Copperfield attic does not simply stop the cooling, it puts water through the ceiling below, and the float switch that should prevent it is missing or disconnected more often than it should be. We check the drain, trap and secondary pan on every attic system we open, regardless of the original complaint.

The mature tree cover that makes Copperfield pleasant also drops a steady load of leaf litter and seed into outdoor condenser coils. A packed coil cannot reject heat, head pressure climbs through the afternoon, and the system either trips its breaker or slowly cooks its own electrical components. A good proportion of the compressor failures we are asked to price in this area started as a coil nobody had rinsed in five years.

My Copperfield house is from the 1980s. Is the ductwork usually the problem?

Often it is a contributing factor. Original flex duct in a Copperfield attic is now around forty years old, and crushed runs, separated connections and degraded insulation are common.
