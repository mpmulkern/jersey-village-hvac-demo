AC Not Cooling in Houston Heat? Causes, Fixes and Costs

An air conditioner that stops cooling in Jersey Village in July is not a maintenance issue, it is a household emergency. Indoor temperatures climb into the 90s within a few hours, and the humidity makes it feel worse than the number suggests. This guide covers what actually causes cooling loss in Houston-area systems, the few checks a homeowner can safely make, what repairs typically cost, and the point at which you should stop troubleshooting and call for emergency service.

Quick Answer: What should I do first?

Turn the system off at the thermostat, check the air filter and confirm the breaker has not tripped. If the filter is clean, the breaker is on, and the unit still will not cool after fifteen minutes, stop there and call a technician. Continuing to run a system with a frozen coil or failing compressor turns a moderate repair into an expensive one.

The Eight Most Common Causes

1. Clogged Air Filter

The cheapest and most common cause. A blocked filter starves airflow across the evaporator coil, which drops capacity and eventually freezes the coil solid.

2. Frozen Evaporator Coil

Ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line. Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. The system must be switched off and thawed before it can be diagnosed.

3. Failed Capacitor

The single most common electrical failure in Houston heat. The unit hums but the compressor or fan will not start. Usually an affordable, same-day fix.

4. Refrigerant Leak

Systems do not consume refrigerant. If it is low, it leaked. Topping up without finding the leak wastes money and vents refrigerant into the atmosphere.

5. Dirty Condenser Coil

The outdoor coil rejects heat. Packed with cottonwood, grass clippings or dust, it cannot, so the system runs constantly and cools poorly.

6. Condensate Drain Backup

A blocked drain line trips the safety float switch and shuts the system down deliberately, to prevent water damage. Very common in Houston humidity.

7. Leaking Ductwork

Cooled air dumped into a 130°F attic never reaches the rooms. The equipment tests fine while the house stays warm.

8. Failed Compressor

The expensive one. On a system past twelve years, compressor failure usually means replacement is the better economic decision.

What You Can Safely Check Yourself

There are exactly four things worth checking before you call, and none of them involve opening the equipment. First, confirm the thermostat is set to cool and the setpoint is genuinely below room temperature; a dead thermostat battery produces a surprising number of service calls. Second, replace the air filter if it is visibly grey or matted. Third, check the breaker panel and the outdoor disconnect for a tripped breaker, and reset it once only. If it trips again, stop and call: repeated tripping indicates an electrical fault and resetting it repeatedly is a genuine fire risk.

Fourth, look at the outdoor unit and the copper lines entering the house. Visible ice means a frozen system: switch it off at the thermostat, leave the fan running to speed the thaw, and expect several hours before anything can be assessed. Beyond these four checks, everything else requires gauges, electrical meters and refrigerant certification. Capacitors in particular hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

Typical Repair Costs in the Houston Area

  • Capacitor replacement: $150–$300, usually same-day
  • Contactor replacement: $150–$350
  • Condensate drain clearing: $150–$300
  • Condenser fan motor: $350–$700
  • Refrigerant leak location and repair: $400–$1,500 depending on location
  • Evaporator coil replacement: $1,200–$2,800
  • Compressor replacement: $1,800–$3,500 — compare against full replacement first

When It Becomes an Emergency

Treat total cooling loss as urgent when the indoor temperature passes the mid-80s and anyone in the house is an infant, elderly, pregnant, or managing a heart or respiratory condition. Pets, particularly flat-faced breeds, are at risk sooner than most people expect. During a Houston heat advisory the interior of a closed house can reach dangerous temperatures within a few hours of the system failing, and overnight offers less relief than it used to.

If you are waiting on a technician, close blinds on sun-facing windows, keep interior doors open so air can move, run ceiling fans in occupied rooms only, and move to the lowest floor. Do not open windows if outdoor conditions are hotter or more humid than inside, which for most of a Houston afternoon they are. Our emergency AC service page covers after-hours response.

Preventing the Next Failure

Most of the no-cool calls we run in July trace back to something that was detectable in March. Capacitors degrade measurably before they fail, refrigerant leaks show up as declining performance, condensate drains clog gradually, and coils foul over a season. A spring maintenance visit catches all four while the weather still allows a scheduled repair instead of an emergency one. Changing the filter every one to three months and keeping two feet clear around the outdoor unit handles most of the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my AC running but not cooling the house?

If the outdoor unit runs and air moves at the vents but nothing gets cold, the usual causes are low refrigerant from a leak, a frozen evaporator coil, a failed compressor, or a dirty condenser coil that cannot reject heat. A clogged filter or leaking ductwork can produce the same symptom by starving the system of airflow.

Should I turn my AC off if it is not cooling?

Yes. Switch the system to off at the thermostat rather than leaving it running. If the coil is frozen, running it further will not help and can damage the compressor. Turning it off also lets ice thaw, which a technician needs before proper diagnosis.

How hot does it have to get before AC failure is an emergency?

In Houston summer conditions, an interior temperature above the mid-80s becomes a genuine health risk for infants, older adults, anyone with a heart or respiratory condition, and pets. During a heat advisory we treat any total loss of cooling as an emergency call regardless of the hour.

What does it cost to fix an AC that is not cooling?

Most repairs land between $150 and $650: capacitors and contactors at the low end, condenser fan motors and refrigerant leak repairs in the middle. A failed compressor runs far higher and, on a system over about twelve years old, usually makes replacement the better value.
