AC Repair vs Replacement in Houston: How to Decide

Almost every homeowner faces this decision in the worst possible circumstances: the house is hot, a technician is standing in the garage with a number, and the choice has to be made now. This guide sets out how the arithmetic actually works in the Houston metro, so you can judge a quote on its merits instead of on how the day is going.

Quick Answer: Repair or replace?

Repair a system under about ten years old with a single failed component. Replace when the compressor has failed, the evaporator coil is leaking on an older unit, the system runs R-22, or you are facing a third significant repair in three seasons. Between those cases, get both numbers in writing and compare them against how many summers the system realistically has left.

Start With Age, Then Adjust for Houston

Manufacturers quote fifteen to twenty years of service life, and national guidance repeats it. That guidance assumes a climate with a real off-season. Our cooling season runs roughly eight months, and in July and August a Jersey Village system may run close to continuously through the afternoon. Local equipment therefore accumulates runtime far faster than the calendar suggests.

In practice, twelve to fifteen years is where we see the balance tip in Harris County. That does not mean a well-maintained fourteen-year-old system should be condemned; it means a significant repair quote at that age deserves the replacement number beside it before you agree to anything.

What Repairs Actually Cost Around Houston

  • Capacitor or contactor: roughly $150–$400, worth doing at almost any system age
  • Condenser fan motor or blower motor: roughly $450–$900, usually worth doing under twelve years
  • Refrigerant leak search and accessible repair with recharge: roughly $400–$900
  • Evaporator coil replacement: roughly $1,200–$2,600, the classic decision point
  • Compressor replacement: roughly $1,800–$3,500, rarely sensible out of warranty
  • Full system replacement: roughly $4,500–$9,500 installed

Put those side by side and the pattern is clear. Cheap electrical parts are always worth fixing. The compressor and coil are where a repair starts costing a meaningful share of a new system that arrives with a full warranty and lower running costs.

The Factors That Change the Answer

Refrigerant type

R-22 has not been produced or imported since 2020. Only reclaimed stock remains, at several times the price of R-410A, so any R-22 system needing a substantial recharge pushes hard toward replacement. Check the data plate on the outdoor unit; systems installed before roughly 2010 are the ones to look at.

Repair history

One failure is bad luck. A third significant repair in three seasons is a pattern, and the rest of the system is the same age as the parts that already failed. Pouring money into a machine that is clearly wearing out is how homeowners end up spending replacement money in instalments, then replacing it anyway.

How well it cooled when it was working

This one gets skipped. If the upstairs never held temperature or the house always felt clammy, the system was wrong or the ducts were leaking. Repairing it restores performance you were never happy with, whereas replacement is a chance to fix the sizing and the ductwork properly.

Warranty status

Check before agreeing to anything. Many compressors and coils carry ten-year parts warranties if the system was registered. Labour is usually yours to pay, but a covered part can turn a replacement-grade quote back into a sensible repair.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Decide

  • What exactly failed, and what caused it — is the root cause airflow, charge or age?
  • Is the part under manufacturer warranty, and was the system registered?
  • What refrigerant does it use, and what would a full recharge cost today?
  • What is the written repair price, and the written replacement price, on the same visit?
  • If we replace, is the existing ductwork adequate, or does it need correction too?

When Replacement Is Clearly Right

Some cases do not need a spreadsheet. A failed compressor on an out-of-warranty system past twelve years is one. A leaking coil on an R-22 unit is another. A system that has had three expensive visits in three summers is a third. In each case the repair leaves you with an old machine and no warranty, at a price that funds a meaningful share of a new one.

The opposite is equally clear: a capacitor on a seven-year-old system is a repair, every time. Nobody should be quoting replacement for that, and if they are, get a second opinion.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age should I stop repairing a Houston AC?

Around twelve to fifteen years is where the balance usually tips here. Houston's long cooling season means local systems accumulate far more runtime per calendar year than the national averages assume, so a twelve-year-old unit in Jersey Village has often done the work of a fifteen-year-old system elsewhere.

Does the 5,000 rule work for deciding?

It is a rough starting point, not an answer. Multiply the system's age in years by the repair quote; if the result exceeds 5,000 it leans toward replacement. It ignores refrigerant type, duct condition and how many repairs the system has already had, so treat it as a sanity check rather than a decision.

My AC still works but the bills keep climbing. Should I replace it?

Not automatically. Rising bills on a system that still cools are often caused by a dirty coil, low charge or leaking ductwork, and all three are cheaper to fix than to replace. Get the airflow and charge verified first, then judge the equipment on what is left.

Is it worth replacing an AC in the middle of a Houston summer?

If the system has genuinely failed, yes, because waiting means weeks in the heat. If it is still running, a planned autumn or winter replacement gives you more time to compare quotes and arrange financing without deciding under pressure.

Will a new system really lower my electricity bill?

Usually yes, though by less than sales figures suggest unless the ductwork is also sound. Moving from an old low-efficiency unit to a modern correctly sized SEER2 system commonly cuts cooling energy use meaningfully, but leaking ducts will give much of that back.
